\begin{abstract}
Quantum computing is usually introduced from the inside out: first qubits, then gates, then an application to justify the machine. This perspective and framework paper reverses that order: a mechanism-first, resource-bounded method for deciding whether a research question suits a quantum solution, and for engineering the algorithm around a real device rather than an imaginary one. The method writes the problem down as a contract---what is computed, how the input is accessed, what the output must be, how much error and failure are tolerable, how the answer is checked, and which classical workflow it must beat---all before a quantum primitive is chosen. End-to-end resource calculations follow, for noisy and for fault-tolerant hardware alike. The hybrid partition is itself an optimization problem: the processor receives only the coherent, reusable kernel whose benefit exceeds the cost of crossing the interface.

The framework then audits proposed framings in DNA-style phase encodings, constrained scheduling, and lattice gauge theory. An appendix executes the whole audit reproducibly on a four-qubit Ising instance---checked against an independent classical oracle and run once, uncorrected, on real hardware. A machine-readable evidence contract seals the claim, allowed variations, baselines, uncertainty sources, and decision rules before results exist, so later outcome-dependent reinterpretation is detectable. One lesson recurs: native quantum dynamics, compact observables, exploitable symmetry, reversible predicates, and structured spectral questions illuminate real opportunity; large data, generic hardness, expensive loading, full-vector outputs, dense embeddings, and unverified heuristics usually do not. The paper proves no new theorem and claims no quantum speedup or advantage. The result is not a catalog of quantum algorithms but a disciplined way to ask whether the quantum work is actually there.
\end{abstract}
